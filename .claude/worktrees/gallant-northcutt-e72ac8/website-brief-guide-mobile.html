Planning Your Website

A plain-English guide to telling the person building your site what you actually want.

Start here
Why this matters

Think of it like building a house

You wouldn't tell a builder "just build me a house" and walk away.

If you don't tell them
They'll guess
And their guess probably isn't what was in your head. You end up redoing rooms you never wanted.
If you do tell them
They'll build it
You get what you pictured because you said what you pictured.
The simple rule
Say more up front
Every answer you give before building saves you from ten "wait, what about…" moments later.

01 · What it's for

Finish this sentence

"I want people to ___ on my website."

Pick the one main thing

Buy something — a product or a service
Book something — an appointment, a table, a call
Contact you — fill out a form, email, or call
Learn something — about you, or what you do
Sign up — for a newsletter or an account

Fuzzy vs. clear

Fuzzy: "I want a website for my coffee shop."
Clear: "I want people nearby to see our menu and book a table."

If you can't say what one thing visitors should do, figure that out first. Everything else depends on it.

02 · Who it's for

Picture one real person

Describe the main visitor like you'd describe a friend.

Tell them
About this person
  • Roughly how old they are
  • Where they live — local or anywhere?
  • What they already know about you
  • Phone user or computer user?
  • In a hurry, or browsing casually?
Tell them
Why they're visiting
  • What brought them to the site
  • What they're hoping to find
  • What would make them trust you
  • What would make them leave
  • Who else they might compare you to

A little story helps a lot. "Sarah, 34, planning her sister's wedding, looking at venues on her phone during her lunch break" tells someone more than any list of numbers.

03 · How it looks

The look and feel

The first thing visitors notice.

The visual stuff

Your logo. Send the original file your designer made — not a screenshot or photo of it.
Your colors. Which colors are "you"? Send examples, or say "pick some that fit."
Photos. Bright and cheerful? Calm and moody? Professional? Homey?
Feel. Modern? Classic? Playful? Serious?

The tone

How do you sound? Friendly, formal, expert, warm? Pick a few words.
Your tagline. The short line that sums up what you do.
Avoid this. Styles, colors, or cheesy stock photos you really don't want.
Not like them. Competitors you want to look different from.
04 · What it says

The actual words and pictures

The hardest part — and the most often skipped.

Send
Real writing
Every headline, paragraph, product description, team bio, and FAQ answer — written out the way you want it to read.
Send
Good photos
The biggest, clearest versions you have. Phone photos are fine if they're good. Fuzzy or tiny pictures look bad on a website.
Send
Permission to use them
Who took the photo? Who wrote the quote? If it's not yours, you need permission before it goes online.
The big one

This is the step most people skip — and it causes the most headaches later. A designer can't know how much room to leave for your "About Us" if you haven't written it yet. When the real words show up later, the whole page often has to be rearranged.

05 · The pages

List every page

Write them out like a table of contents.

Home
About Us
↳ Our Team
↳ Our Story
What We Do
↳ Service A
↳ Service B
Our Work
Blog
Contact

For every page, answer

  • Why is this page here?
  • Who lands on it?
  • What should they click next?
  • Is it needed now, or can it wait?

A list of 8 actual pages beats "a normal website" every single time.

06 · What it does

The clickable stuff

Anything more than just reading.

Common things people ask for

Contact forms. What should people fill in? Where should the message go?
Online store. How many products? How do people pay?
Booking. Appointments, reservations, classes — how does it work?
Accounts. Do visitors log in? What can they do once they're in?

For each thing, describe

What the visitor puts in. Their name, email, date, etc.
What happens next. They see a thank-you page, get an email, etc.
What if something goes wrong. What do they see if they mess up?
What you see on your end. An email? A list in a dashboard?

Say it like a little story: "A customer fills in their name, email, and message, then they get a confirmation email, and I get a notification."

07 · Examples

Sites you love, sites you don't

And why for both.

Love
Three sites you like
  • Send the actual web addresses
  • Say exactly what you like — the colors? The layout? The photos?
  • Be specific. "The homepage" is better than "the whole thing"
Dislike
Three sites you don't
  • What made you close the tab?
  • Too busy? Too plain? Hard to read? Confusing?
  • What you don't want is just as useful to know
Surprising
A site from a different field
  • Run a bakery but love a law firm's site? Say so
  • It helps your site stand out
  • Often the most useful example you'll give

09 · What "done" means

Know your finish line

What has to happen for the website to count as a win?

Pick something you can check

Number of calls or emails coming in each week
Bookings or sales made through the site
New sign-ups to your mailing list
Where you are today so you can see the change

Or just be honest

Sometimes the goal isn't a number. Sometimes it's:

"I need a site I'm not embarrassed to share."

"I want to look legit when I hand out business cards."

That totally counts. Just say it out loud. A clear reason beats a made-up number.

Watch out

Things that go wrong

The stuff that slows projects down over and over. Tap to expand.

01 · "Make it look like [famous company]"

Big companies have huge teams working on their sites for years. Instead of pointing to the whole thing, pick the one specific part you like — the colors, the photos, the way the menu works. That's something someone can actually build.

02 · "We'll figure out the words later"

The words shape how everything looks. If they show up after the site is designed, whole sections usually have to be rebuilt. Write them first. It feels slower but actually saves time.

03 · Adding new things halfway through

"Oh, can we also add a blog? And a newsletter? And a chat thing?" Every addition after the plan is set costs more than if it had been there from the start. Put everything on the list up front. Then cut what you don't need.

04 · Too many people giving opinions

When five people give feedback, they usually disagree with each other. Pick one person to make final decisions. Others can weigh in, but only one voice says "yes, ship it."
